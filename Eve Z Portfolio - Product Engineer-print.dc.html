Research direction

Designing AI-Assisted Workplaces

The people who understand a workflow best are often not the people who design its software. Experienced professionals must therefore adapt their work to rigid systems, move manually among disconnected platforms, and rely on spreadsheets or informal procedures to fill the gaps.

I am interested in AI-supported end-user development: enabling domain professionals to create and customize workplace tools without becoming traditional software engineers. Generative AI could help workers translate situated expertise into prompts, rules, examples, and reusable workflows. Designers, engineers, and IT teams would continue to provide secure infrastructure, evaluation mechanisms, shared standards, and organizational governance.

My central research question: How can domain professionals create and continually improve AI-assisted workplace tools while organizations preserve security, interoperability, provenance, consistency, and accountability?

01

Professionals as Toolmakers

How can experienced workers translate tacit knowledge into customizable, prompt-generated tools? How should interfaces help nonprogrammers inspect, test, and correct what AI creates?

02

Coordinated Access Across Fragmented Systems

How can an interface bring authorized information from disconnected systems into a coordinated workspace without indiscriminately centralizing sensitive data?

03

Verifiable Human–AI Collaboration

How should AI retrieve, compare, and draft while making evidence, uncertainty, and limitations visible — and preserving human authority over consequential decisions?

Autonomy principle

The system may monitor, retrieve, compare, and draft. The professional verifies, corrects, approves, and acts.

Deployed operational workflow

Detector Data Quality Control

Traffic engineers must reconcile detector volumes, identifiers, dictionary mappings, movements, and model baselines — where missing data and incorrect mappings quietly produce misleading conclusions. I translated that engineer-led review into a browser-based QC workflow that harmonizes schemas, checks missing or low-coverage samples, flags zero volumes and spikes, identifies mapping gaps, and supports approach-level review. In the workflow where it was applied, review time decreased from approximately 2.5 hours to 10 minutes per intersection — about a 93% reduction.

Independent human-in-the-loop AI prototype

Traffic Report Agent — From Raw Detector Data to Verifiable AI-Assisted Review

Extends the deterministic QC workflow. It works from validated QC results rather than unconstrained raw logs, retrieves documentation, QC rules, and approved examples, drafts structured review notes, and screens them with a separate LLM-based evaluator before requiring engineer approval to export. Approved corrections are preserved with provenance and become retrievable context — the model is not retrained. Pipeline: deterministic QC → context assembly → evidence retrieval → structured draft → output evaluation → engineer review → approved-example retrieval. Implemented prototype; evaluation in progress. The QC time reduction above is not attributable to this prototype.

Product discovery and accountability concept

Making Operational Changes Traceable

Configuration changes made in operational infrastructure can be difficult to detect and reconstruct. When a problem is discovered later, engineers may know that a setting is incorrect without knowing when it changed, through which access path, or why. I identified the problem through frontline operational experience, distinguished change detection from attribution, separated the core user need from optional integrations, and developed a software-first product framing. Research insight: detection, attribution, authorization, and accountability are related, but they are not interchangeable. The concept received positive cross-functional review and advanced beyond initial discovery for further product and technical consideration.

Independent multi-agent research prototype

DOT Data Gap Researcher

Inventories public transportation data resources, compares observed coverage against documented expectations, coordinates specialized retrieval and analysis steps, and produces evidence-linked gap reports for human review. Each finding carries the evidence behind it so a reader can verify it independently; the deterministic catalog diff runs before any model synthesis. Open source: github.com/zhaxinge/dot-data-gap-researcher

Field-informed research direction

Coordinating Decisions Across Fragmented Operational Systems

During an incident, an operator may need to consult email, camera systems, traffic-alert platforms, mapping services, asset databases, and maintenance-ticket systems. Different agencies and vendors control these sources under separate permissions and data-sharing agreements, so the operator becomes the integration layer — locating information, reconciling inconsistencies, judging recency, and remembering what has already been checked. The opportunity is a federated coordination interface, not a centralized database: one that preserves access permissions, source attribution, recency, conflicting reports, and the distinction between human reports, retrieved records, and AI inferences.

About

How I got here

I came to operational engineering through design and then through quantitative research — an unusual route that turned out to be the useful one. Landscape architecture at Beijing Forestry University taught me to read a system as relationships among people, institutions, and infrastructure. Graduate work in urban spatial analytics taught me to test claims against data instead of accepting them.

Transportation operations then put me next to the people doing the work: engineers reconciling detector data across a large signal network, operators piecing together an incident from six systems that do not talk to each other. Watching skilled professionals absorb the friction their software created is what moved me from analysis toward building, and from building toward research.
